# Pitch guide

How Claude writes every pitch in `pitches/<show-slug>.md`. One show, one pitch. Never a mail merge.

## The one principle
The best pitch does not lead with the guest's credentials. It leads with value to the host's listeners.
The host is always asking one question: why would my listeners care? Answer it before they ask.

## Where the words come from
- **You.** The only source for who the guest is, what they know and how they talk is `profile/guest-profile.md`.
  Re-read it before every pitch.
- **Their words, quoted.** Lift phrases straight from the profile: the way they describe who they help, the
  change they make, their stories. Light edits for grammar only. If the profile says "without being told to just
  do more kegels", the pitch says that, not "without outdated advice".
- **The show.** Its description and recent episode titles from `shortlist/shortlist.json`. If you can read the
  show's public website or listen notes, use one specific detail from it.
- **Nothing else.** No invented numbers, clients, awards or results. Proof only comes from "Proof you can show".
  If a claim is not in the profile, it is not in the pitch.

## What every pitch file contains
Use this order and these headings.

```
# Pitch: <Show name>
Show: <title>  |  Score: <n>/100  |  Where to send: <public guest page or show website>
Drafted: <YYYY-MM-DD>  |  Status: draft, not sent

## Subject lines
1. Direct: says the topic plainly.
2. Curiosity: "the next conversation" (see below).
3. Value-led: what the listener walks away with.

## Email
<the pitch body, 150 to 220 words>

## Three talking points
1. <point>: <why it matters to this show's listeners>
2. ...
3. ...

## Short bio (under 75 words)
<third person, built from "How you like to be introduced">

## Why this show, why now
<two or three sentences for the guest, not the host>

## Follow-up nudge (send only if no reply in 7 days)
<three to five sentences>
```

## The email, part by part
1. **Opening, one sentence.** Prove you listened. Name a specific episode, guest or theme from their recent titles.
   Never "I love your podcast" or "huge fan".
2. **Why this guest for these listeners, one short paragraph.** Connect what the guest does (profile: who you
   help, what changes) to what this audience is living with right now.
3. **What is different about this take, one sentence.** Name the guest's angle plainly, so the host knows why
   this guest and not the next person who does the same thing.
4. **The episode idea, one or two sentences.** A named, specific conversation this audience has not heard yet.
   Not "I could talk about running". Yes: "Why the six-week check is a starting line, not a finish line."
   Pull it from "Three stories you can tell".
5. **The three talking points.** Concrete things a listener walks away with. Each one tied to this show.
6. **The ask, one line.** One clear ask, no menu of options, no over-explaining. Then sign off with the name and
   "Where listeners can find you".

## The "next conversation" subject line
Look at the host's recent titles. If the guest's idea extends, completes or complicates an episode they already
made, the curiosity line names that link: "The episode after <their episode title>" or "What <their episode>
left your listeners asking". It proves the guest listened, which is the strongest credential at this stage.
If nothing in their archive connects, write a plain curiosity line instead. Never fake the link.

## When the show is bigger than the guest
- Judge fit on what the guest and the show care about, not on audience size.
- Never apologise for the size gap in the pitch.
- If the guest names their own audience in the bio, state the number once, plainly.
- If the gap needs a word at all, it goes in "Why this show, why now", for the guest's eyes only.

## When the show serves a specific community
Keep the guest's idea intact. Show why it is the missing piece in the conversation that show already has.
Do not bolt on identity framing the guest did not use in their own profile.

## Hard lines
- Respect "Topics you refuse". If the show is built on one of them, say so and do not draft.
- No invented familiarity with the host. No "as we discussed", no first-name chumminess the guest has not earned.
- No template voice: no "I hope this email finds you well", "I'd love to pick your brain", "value bomb",
  "unlock", "game-changer", "synergy", "reach out", "circle back".
- No em dashes. They read as machine-written. Use a full stop or a comma.
- Short sentences. One idea each. Read it aloud in the guest's voice before you save it.
- Never send. Never look up or guess an email address. The "To" line stays empty unless the guest gives it.

## The follow-up nudge
- First nudge 7 days after the pitch. Second nudge 10 days after that. Then stop. Two nudges is the ceiling.
- Reply in the same thread. Do not re-pitch from the top.
- Add one new thing: a fresh episode of theirs you noticed, or a second episode idea. Never "just bumping this".
- Three to five sentences. Warm, certain, easy to say yes or no to.

## Quality bar (check before you save)
- [ ] Could this email only have been sent to this show? If you could swap the show name, rewrite it.
- [ ] Does the first line name something real from their recent episodes?
- [ ] Are at least two phrases lifted from the guest profile?
- [ ] Is every claim backed by "Proof you can show"?
- [ ] One ask, no em dashes, 150 to 220 words in the email body?
- [ ] Bio under 75 words?
- [ ] Status says "draft, not sent"?
